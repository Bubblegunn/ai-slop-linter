# A Hindi rule set, and why none of it shipped

Research by @shivam-070208, September 2026. Issue #1.

## What was asked, and what came back

The goal of this research was to find a Hindi-specific writing pattern that
could be added as a deterministic rule to the linter.

The rule needed to describe a characteristic of machine-generated Hindi rather
than simply being a phrase that is common in Hindi. It also needed to have a
published source that could support the rule and enough separation from
human-written text to be useful in a linter.

I tested several types of patterns against the CT² Hindi corpus, including
frequent phrases, common constructions, repeated sentence structures, exact
sentence repetition, cross-entity templates, and lexical redundancy.

Several patterns looked interesting when comparing human and machine-generated
text. However, closer inspection showed three recurring problems:

- some candidates were ordinary Hindi expressions that humans use naturally;
- some candidates were influenced by the topic or structure of the articles in
  the corpus;
- some of the strongest signals were specific to particular models rather than
  being a general characteristic of machine-generated Hindi.

Because of these problems, none of the candidates provided enough evidence to
justify adding a Hindi lint rule.

This is a negative result about the methods tested against this corpus. It does
not mean that Hindi has no AI-writing tells, only that none of the candidates
investigated here survived the validation needed for a deterministic rule.

## The corpus and the method

The main corpus used for this research was the CT² Hindi dataset. It contains
human-written Hindi articles together with Hindi text generated by several
language models.

The dataset contains 7,043 source articles from BBC Hindi and NDTV. The
available generated responses include outputs from BARD, GPT-3.5, GPT-4,
Gemma-2B, and Gemma-7B, although some Gemma outputs are missing from the
dataset.

The human articles were compared with generated responses associated with the
same source articles where possible. This paired setup helped reduce some of
the topic differences that would appear if human and machine texts were
compared as completely independent collections.

The research was performed in several stages.

### Phrase frequency

The first experiments searched for Hindi words and n-grams that occurred more
frequently in generated text than in human text.

Counts were normalised per 1,000 words because the human articles and generated
responses have different average lengths. The results were then compared
across the BBC Hindi and NDTV portions of the corpus and across the available
models.

Frequency was treated only as a way of generating candidates. A phrase was not
considered a rule simply because it had a high human-to-machine frequency
ratio.

### Context validation

Candidate phrases were inspected in their surrounding sentences.

This was important because a phrase can be statistically associated with
generated text without being an AI-writing tell. For example, a phrase may
occur more often because generated articles discuss a particular subject more
often, or because the phrase is simply a common construction in Hindi.

Candidates that looked strong statistically but were normal Hindi usage were
rejected at this stage.

### Structural patterns

After the phrase-based experiments, the research moved to larger structures.

I tested repeated sentence openings, repeated list-item structures, exact
sentence repetition, similar sentence templates with different entities, and
lexical overlap between adjacent sentences.

The purpose was to see whether a structural pattern could provide a stronger
signal than individual words or phrases.

These experiments also required contextual inspection because repetition and
parallel sentence structures can occur naturally in news articles, lists,
recipes, sports reporting, and other structured writing.

## The candidates, and what killed each one

### Phrase and n-gram candidates

Several candidates initially showed substantially higher rates in generated
text, including:

- `एक महत्वपूर्ण`
- `का उपयोग`
- `के खिलाफ`
- `में सक्षम`
- `करने में मदद`
- `का निर्णय`
- `की आवश्यकता`
- `एक नया`
- `इस प्रकार`
- `एक प्रमुख`

For example, `एक महत्वपूर्ण` appeared much more frequently in generated text
than in human text in both BBC Hindi and NDTV.

For the BBC portion, the phrase occurred in approximately 2.10% of human
documents and 13.20% of generated documents in the document-level analysis,
with an average model ratio of about 6.28x.

For NDTV, the difference was even larger: approximately 0.53% of human
documents compared with 8.05% of generated documents, with an average ratio of
about 15.19x across the available models.

Despite the large difference, the phrase itself is normal Hindi. It can be
used naturally when describing an important event, decision, development, or
other subject.

This made it unsuitable as a lint rule. A frequency difference alone could
not establish that the phrase was an AI-writing characteristic.

Other candidates had similar problems.

For example, `का उपयोग` showed a strong difference in the BBC data, while
`के खिलाफ`, `का निर्णय`, `में सक्षम`, and `करने में मदद` also showed elevated
rates in generated text. Context inspection showed that these expressions
were commonly used in ordinary Hindi news writing.

Some candidates were also affected by the subjects covered by the corpus.
Expressions related to decisions, opposition, or the use of something are
expected to occur in news articles regardless of whether the text was written
by a human or generated by a model.

These candidates were therefore rejected as ordinary-language or
topic-dependent patterns.

### Construction-level patterns

The next experiment looked at larger constructions rather than individual
phrases.

One candidate was:

`एक चुनौती है`

along with related constructions such as:

`एक चुनौती हो सकती है`

The generated text contained more examples of some of these constructions,
but contextual inspection showed that they were normal ways of describing a
problem or situation in Hindi.

Other constructions involving words such as `महत्वपूर्ण`, `मजबूत`, and
`प्रमुख` showed similar behaviour.

Some differences were also concentrated in individual models. A pattern that
was strong in one model did not necessarily appear in the other generated
outputs.

The candidate constructions were therefore rejected because they did not
provide evidence of a general Hindi AI-writing characteristic.

### Structural repetition

The next experiments looked for repeated structures within a document.

Repeated sentence openings and repeated list structures appeared much more
often in some generated outputs than in the human articles. BARD and the
Gemma models showed particularly strong repetition-related signals.

However, the behaviour was not consistent across models. GPT-3.5 and GPT-4
showed substantially less of the same behaviour.

Context inspection also showed that some repetition came from normal document
structures. Lists, recipes, sports reporting, and similar formats can
naturally contain sentences with the same structure.

This meant that a rule based only on repeated openings or list structures
would likely produce false positives on normal human writing.

### Exact sentence repetition

Exact sentence repetition produced one of the strongest statistical
differences in the experiments.

For example, in the NDTV portion of the corpus, exact repetition occurred in
approximately:

| Source | Documents with repeated sentences |
| --- | ---: |
| Human | 0.25% |
| BARD | 17.46% |
| GPT-3.5 | 0.21% |
| GPT-4 | 0% |
| Gemma-2B | 12.97% |
| Gemma-7B | 20.38% |

The difference was large for BARD and the Gemma models. However, the same
pattern was almost absent from GPT-3.5 and GPT-4.

Inspection of the repeated examples showed that many were exact duplication
artefacts rather than a general characteristic of Hindi AI writing.

Because the behaviour was strongly model-dependent, raw sentence repetition
was rejected as a general Hindi rule.

### Cross-entity templates

I also tested whether generated text reused similar sentence structures while
changing the main entity.

For example, several sentences could follow approximately the same structure
while replacing a person, team, organisation, place, or other entity.

Some generated examples looked promising because several sentences followed
very similar structures. However, similar patterns were also found in
human-written news articles.

This was especially common in sports, politics, election reporting, and
statistical writing, where repeating a sentence structure for different
entities is a normal writing technique.

The similarity measure could therefore identify a repeated structure, but it
could not reliably determine whether that structure came from AI generation.

The experiment was useful for finding possible candidates, but none of the
templates were stable enough to become a rule.

### Lexical redundancy

The final experiment looked for adjacent sentences with high lexical overlap.

The hypothesis was that generated text might sometimes state an idea and then
immediately repeat or restate it.

This produced a noticeable signal for some models, particularly BARD and
Gemma. However, exact duplicate sentences accounted for a large part of the
result.

After inspecting the examples, the remaining overlaps included normal
reformulations, captions, list structures, and other document-specific
patterns.

The signal also varied significantly between models. GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 showed
far less of the behaviour than some of the other models.

Because the observed signal was largely explained by duplication and
model-specific behaviour, lexical redundancy was rejected as a general Hindi
rule.

## What would have changed the answer

A Hindi rule could be added if a candidate met the requirements used throughout
this research.

First, there would need to be a published source supporting the pattern as an
AI-writing characteristic. A phrase being more common in generated text is
not enough by itself.

Second, the pattern would need to show useful separation between human and
machine-written Hindi while keeping false positives on normal Hindi writing
low enough for a linter.

A stronger evaluation would ideally use human and generated Hindi from multiple
domains and multiple language models. The human side should also contain enough
variety to test whether the candidate occurs naturally in different forms of
Hindi writing.

Pre-2021 human-written Hindi would be particularly useful for checking whether
a candidate is actually associated with recent machine-generated writing
rather than simply reflecting a change in writing style.

A candidate that continued to separate human and generated text under these
conditions, while also having an appropriate published source, could be
considered for a future `hi/...` rule.

## What this says, and what it does not

This research does not show that Hindi has no AI-writing tells.

It shows that, using the methods tested here and the CT² Hindi corpus, none of
the candidates produced enough evidence to justify adding a deterministic Hindi
rule to the linter.

A pattern being more common in generated text is not sufficient by itself. It
also needs to be specific enough that the rule does not simply flag normal
Hindi writing.

The experiments demonstrated several ways in which a seemingly strong
candidate can be misleading:

- ordinary Hindi expressions can have higher frequencies in generated text;
- article topics can create large differences between human and generated
  text;
- repetition can be caused by the behaviour of a particular model rather than
  by a general property of AI-generated Hindi.

The result is therefore a negative result about the tested methods and corpus,
not a general claim about Hindi or about all Hindi-generated text.

No `hi/...` rule was added because the available evidence was not strong enough
to support one.

## Reproducing it

The experiments were run against the CT² Hindi dataset using the human articles
and the available generated responses from the different models.

The analysis was performed in several stages:

1. Tokenise and normalise the Hindi text.
2. Compare phrase and n-gram frequencies between human and generated text.
3. Normalise frequency results per 1,000 words.
4. Compare results across BBC Hindi and NDTV.
5. Compare human and generated texts associated with the same source article
   where possible.
6. Inspect high-ranking candidates in their surrounding context.
7. Test structural repetition, sentence templates, and lexical redundancy.
8. Reject candidates that could be explained by ordinary Hindi usage, topic
   effects, or model-specific generation behaviour.

The dataset and analysis scripts are not part of this repository, so the full
analysis cannot currently be reproduced from the repository alone.

The results documented here are the measurements obtained during this research.
Where an exact measurement was not retained, the candidate is described as
having been rejected during contextual validation rather than being given a
reconstructed number.

Future research can reproduce or extend these experiments by using the CT²
Hindi corpus and applying the same comparison and validation process.
